# Concepts, hooks and structure

## What the creative decides
- **[M] The creative is the biggest lever an advertiser controls, but the famous number has context.** A market-research firm attributed ~49% of ad-driven sales lift to creative (2017, repeated 2023 across ~450 consumer-packaged-goods campaigns). Grocery products, TV and digital, not direct-response info-products. Direction, not a target.
- **[C] With automated delivery the creative does what targeting used to.** Meta says varying creatives in theme, message and visual is now the main way to reach different audiences, and its retrieval system (Andromeda, Dec 2024) was built for many more creatives per account. (Meta for Business, 22/04/2025; Meta Engineering, 02/12/2024; read 13/11/2026)
- **[C] Creative does not fix offer or page.** High CTR with low conversion is almost always an ad promise the page does not repeat. Check price, page and checkout before remaking creatives.

## Angles that recur in any niche (choose by diagnosis, not taste)
Pain and identification; mechanism (why it works); demonstration (the product doing it); quantity and value; routine (how it fits the day); common enemy (the alternative that failed, no brand named); candour (what the product is not); verifiable social proof; offer and real deadline; gift and calendar date; mini-lesson (teaches something useful in 10 s).

- **[F] Effort split:** most effort on variations of concepts that already sell, less on new concepts, a little on odd bets. The 70/20/10 figures that circulate have no measured origin.
- **[F] Organic and paid feed each other.** What retains organically tends to work in paid, and home-made formats tend to beat commercial-looking pieces in vertical feeds; practitioner and UGC-vendor reports, no public randomised test.

## Hooks: the first 3 seconds
- **[C]** Proposition in the first 3 s, hook resolved by 6 s (TikTok "Creative best practices"); get to the core quickly, tight framing, lively pace, brand from the start (Google "ABCDs of effective video ads"). Read 13/11/2026.
- **[M]** An old platform/measurement-firm study (2015) found up to 47% of campaign value (recall, awareness, intent) in the first 3 s and up to 74% in the first 10 s. Brand metrics, 2015 feed. Supports "say what it is early", not "the rest does not matter".
- **Three layers say the same at the same instant:** image (frame 0), on-screen text (large, from the first frame) and voice. Frame 0 is also the thumbnail.
- **Honest hook types, safest to riskiest:** a question about a common situation; a real product number; a demonstration already in progress; a counter-intuitive claim the video proves; a challenge to the viewer ("you have 7 seconds"); the objection said aloud ("where's the catch?"); mini-lesson ("X does not mean Y").
- **[F] Hook authors, with the critique.** Popular books argue that the short expectation-breaking idea wins, and that an ad = call-out (who should pay attention) + value + action, with "more, then better, then new". Useful scaffolding. Limits: they optimise attention and volume with examples from their own firms and big budgets (survivorship bias); "the hook is 80% of the ad" is a slogan, not a measure; aggressive call-outs ("stop scrolling if you…") read as scams to sceptical audiences and collide with personal-attribute policies and EU law. One vendor's 2026 data had the category with the best hook rate 12th of 14 in ROAS.

## Video structure
**[F] Direct-response skeleton:** hook → problem → mechanism or demonstration → proof → offer → action. Scaffolding; no formula is proven superior.

| Awareness | Open with | Body | Close with | Typical length |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| unaware / problem-aware (cold) | situation or pain | why it happens + mechanism with product on screen | small step (test, sample, "see how it works") | 15-30 s |
| solution-aware | mechanism or demonstration | product in use, counts | product page | 15-25 s |
| product-aware / most aware (retargeting) | offer, objection or real deadline | 2-3 short answers | purchase | 6-15 s |

- **[C] Product early and in use;** "make the product the ad; be exact and tangible; ask for action after context" (Google guidance for action objectives). Demonstration is the oldest proof in advertising.
- **[C] Ask clearly, on screen and in voice;** the ad's button label and the end card say the same thing.
- **[M] Slippery slide:** each sentence exists so the next is heard; each cut leaves a question the next answers. Short sentences, one idea each.
- **Duration is a test variable.** TikTok does not fix duration in the auction and suggests 9-15 s for its reserved format [search]; Meta accepts 0 s to 15 min in Reels. The colder the audience and newer the mechanism, the more seconds needed. The video lasts as long as it takes to say the single idea with proof on screen, not a cut more. Make a 6-10 s version of every winner.
- **Rhythm:** a cut or motion every 1-3 s (slow zoom, counting number). Text meant to be read stays still and whole for ~0.3 s per word after it appears. TikTok mentions 5-10 words per second for on-screen text; in languages with long words that is too fast. When in doubt, slower.
- **End card:** logo, one phrase, button drawn like the ad's label, the address. 2-4 s, no new information, inside the safe zone.

## Formats

| Format | Good for | Care |
|---|---|---|
| Screen demo (real recording of app/product) | proof for sceptics; problem/solution-aware | real screen; sample data flagged; visible taps |
| Clean motion graphics | mechanism, numbers, comparison | not a slide deck; real product inside |
| Home-made (hand holding product, phone in hand, desk) | looks like content, not a commercial | never pretend to be a customer |
| Person talking to camera | trust and pace | only a real person with an image-rights contract; never a persona/avatar as a real human |
| Mini-lesson | reciprocity; works organic and paid | content reviewed by a specialist |
| Static 4:5 / 1:1 | cheap tests of angle, name, audience; retargeting with offer | one phrase + real product + button |
| Carousel | sequence (problem → mechanism → product → offer); honest comparison | card 1 is the hook; each card stands alone |
| Stories 9:16 | retargeting, real deadline, gift | one phrase, one image, one tap |

- **[F] Images test, video scales:** statics cheaply find which angle or name pulls clicks and purchases; the winner becomes video. [1-SRC practitioner report]
- **[M] People help tell the story** (Google: "show people"; Shorts: "let talent be real"). Without an authorised real person, hands, voice and screen work without inventing anyone.

## Book-derived rules (principles in my own words; citations and verification status in `BIBLIOGRAPHY.md`)
1. **Distinctive brand cues (Sharp, *How Brands Grow*, 2010; Romaniuk, *Building Distinctive Brand Assets*, 2018) [M]:** reuse a recognisable color, shape, sound or character across ads so a viewer knows whose ad it is in under a second; consistency compounds across batches. Do not rotate the whole look every test.
2. **Emotion for the long term, rational offer for now (Binet and Field, *The Long and the Short of It*, 2013) [M]:** emotional storytelling builds future demand; direct, rational offers drive immediate sales. Mix them in the batch: brand-led concepts and offer-led concepts judged by different metrics (view and engagement vs cost per acquisition).
3. **Hook, story, offer (Brunson, *Expert Secrets*, 2017) [F practitioner]:** a short sequence for scripts: stop the scroll, give a believable story that fits the viewer's situation, then a clear offer. Use only true stories and real results; fake testimonial scripts are banned (see `policy-and-law.md`).
4. **Offer clarity (Hormozi, *$100M Offers*, 2021) [F practitioner]:** state the result, the time and the effort in the ad in plain numbers, within what the page can prove; price and deadline wording per `offers`.
5. **Story in 15-60 seconds (Gallo, *The Storyteller's Secret*, 2016, lessons of ch. 5, 9, 11, 13, 18, 19, 22, 23) [F practitioner]:** a short ad story needs a viewer-like person who wants something, an obstacle or antagonist (the problem or the old way, ch. 9 and 22), a struggle and a turn (ch. 5), and a result where the product is the tool, not the hero. Add one concrete, true detail from real use (ch. 13), a first line that carries the whole idea (ch. 18), three beats (ch. 19), plain words, and a story that can be told in about a minute (ch. 23). A small honest surprise earns the next second (ch. 11); an analogy explains an unfamiliar feature faster than a spec (ch. 21). Checks: the viewer is the hero; the story is true (no invented origin or customer, see `policy-and-law.md`). The book leans on oxytocin and cortisol claims to say stories "work"; that link is popular science and not evidence for ad results, so run story vs no-story as a normal concept test in the batch.
6. **Make the product do the job inside the scene (Lindstrom, *Buyology*, 2008, ch. 2) [M, contested]:** the author's own TV study found brands woven into the show's plot were remembered, while logos and spots that did not play a part were forgotten (and a heavy sponsor could crowd out a rival's recall). It rests on one commissioned study that is not independently published. Low-risk use: in UGC and native video show the product solving the problem on screen instead of a logo overlay or a floating pack shot, and test integrated vs overlay versions.
7. **Neuromarketing claims to leave out (Lindstrom, ch. 3 and 4) [X/contested]:** do not plan creative around "mirror neurons make people want what they see", subliminal messages, or a brain scan read as "love" of a brand. The book itself recounts that the famous 1950s cinema test of hidden "drink Coke" frames was admitted by its author to be invented, and then leans on word- and face-priming studies, the kind of effect that has replicated poorly. Reverse inference from a scan to a feeling was widely criticised by neuroscientists, human mirror-neuron claims are debated, and subliminal techniques are restricted in EU audiovisual advertising (Directive 2010/13/EU, not re-fetched this pass). What survives without the brain talk: show real people using the product, keep sound and picture consistent, and make each sensory cue (voice, music, colour) belong to the brand and the story. Ch. 8 (selling to the senses) was not read in full.

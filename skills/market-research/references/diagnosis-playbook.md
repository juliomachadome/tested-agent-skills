# Diagnosis playbook: market research

Use when evidence looks good but sales do not, or when a signal is being over-read. Tags: **[C]** consensus, **[M]** serious but single or correlational, **[F]** practitioner.

## Step 0: is the evidence trustworthy
Answer in this order before any tree.
1. **What exactly was observed**: raw counts and dates, not "great response". Write numerator and denominator (30 signups out of 500 visits, not "30 signups").
2. **Behaviour or words**: did anyone spend money, time, reputation or switching effort? Ladder, weakest to strongest **[C]**: like or view, comment, free signup, qualified signup (answered a question), booked call, deposit or pre-order, paid price, repeat purchase.
3. **Who is the sample**: your followers, friends, the people a viral video reached, or strangers matching the segment? Only the last generalises.
4. **Who benefits from the answer**: a seller of a tool, a friend, you. Discount accordingly.
5. **Period**: spike, season or trend? A single week says little; compare with last year and with adjacent terms.
6. **Sample size**: under about 30 buyers or 100 survey answers, a rate has a wide interval. With 5 buyers out of 100 visits the plausible true rate is roughly 2 to 11 percent (Wilson 95 percent). State the range, not the point.

If Step 0 fails, the finding is a hypothesis; say so and run a cheaper behavioural test.

## Signals that mislead
| Signal | Why it misleads | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Vanity interest (views, likes, follows) | Attention is cheap; it measures the hook, not the need **[C]** | Count saves, shares, "how" comments, and clicks to a page with a price |
| Likes vs purchases | Many likes, few buyers is normal; the gap is the real conversion | Measure click to page, page to signup, signup to pay separately |
| Friends and family praise | Kind, unpaid, not the segment | Ask for a pre-order or an introduction to a stranger who has the problem |
| Survey intent vs behaviour | People overstate what they would do; intent surveys overstate sales **[M]** | Use surveys to bracket a range, test with money |
| Small samples | A handful of responses swings wildly | Report ranges; extend the test before betting big |
| Seasonality | A January spike for fitness is not growth | Look at 3-5 years of Trends and compare same months |
| Survivorship | You see the winners that stayed visible | Look for failed competitors and dead launches too |
| Leading questions | "Wouldn't it be great if ..." yields yes | Ask about past behaviour and spend (Mom Test, `product-management`) |
| Selection of the audience | Your own audience already likes you | Test with cold traffic or a community that does not know you |

## Tree A: "people say they want it but nobody pays"
1. Check the ask: did you show a price and a buy button, or only ask opinions? If no price was shown, no WTP evidence exists. Run a priced pre-sale.
2. Check the problem: is it painful, frequent and already costing money or time? A mild annoyance gets praise, not payment. Ask what they do today and what it costs them (alternative in `SKILL.md` section 1).
3. Check the buyer: the person who likes it may not hold the budget (employee vs employer, child vs parent).
4. Check price vs value: if the stated WTP range (`sizing-and-wtp.md`) is below your price, the idea may not clear unit economics.
5. Check trust: unknown seller, no proof. Add a refund promise, proof, a sample.
6. Check the alternative: a free option that is good enough. Then the gap must be named and large.
Decision: if after a priced test with at least 100 qualified visitors nobody pays, pivot the segment or the problem, not the copy.

## Tree B: "waitlist is big, conversion is tiny"
Numbers first: waitlist 2,000, launch day buyers 12 is 0.6 percent. Typical launch conversion varies widely by list quality and price; measure your own **[F]**.
1. **Source quality**: giveaway, free tool or broad viral traffic produces cold lists. Compare by source if tagged.
2. **Cost of entry**: signup was free and unqualified. Next time add one qualifying question or a small deposit.
3. **Time decay**: long wait cools interest. Mail early, ship a small first item.
4. **Offer mismatch**: the lead magnet attracted a different need than the paid product. Re-read signups' stated problem.
5. **Price shock**: first price seen at checkout. Show an expected range during the wait.
6. **Mechanics**: emails in spam, no reminder, broken checkout. Check delivery and a test purchase.
Next test: segment the list by qualifier answer and open rate; sell to the top 10 percent first with a pre-sale.

## Tree C: "a competitor exists, so there is no market?"
Reverse: competitors with revenue prove demand **[C]**. Check:
1. Traction is real (reviews over time, ads running months, hiring, pricing changes) or only a landing page.
2. What customers complain about in 1-3 star reviews (gaps) and praise in 5 star (the language to use).
3. Whether segment, price, format, language, country or speed is underserved. In Brazil a strong foreign product with no Portuguese support, local payment (Pix, boleto) or local examples is a gap.
4. Whether the incumbent is too big to care about your niche.
If you find no gap customers pay to close, go no-go or pick a narrower segment. If there is no competitor at all, find out why: no demand, no way to reach buyers, or a failed market.

## Tree D: "no search volume"
1. Is the problem named in the customers' words? Try synonyms, questions, the pain phrase rather than your solution name.
2. Does the need live outside search: marketplaces, YouTube, TikTok, Instagram, WhatsApp groups, forums, comments, app stores? Check there.
3. Is the category new (people do not search for what they do not know exists)? Then volume will be low; demand must be created or found in the problem, not the solution.
4. Trends shows relative interest only and hides low volumes; use it for shape and comparison, not for absolute counts **[C]** (Google Trends help).
5. B2B or niche markets have tiny volume with high value: 200 searches a month can support a business at a high price.
Decision: low search plus strong community asks plus pre-sale is valid; low search plus no asks anywhere is a no-go.

## Tree E: "viral interest fades"
1. Classify the shape: spike (days), season, growth. A viral wave loses most attention in days **[F]**.
2. Separate curiosity from need: comments that ask "how did you do it", "where do I get", "template please" are need; laughs and shares are not.
3. If need is real and the window is short, sell a fast format now (checklist, template, mini-guide, workshop) as a pre-sale with a delivery date inside the window (`viral-content` for the path from views to sales).
4. If the wave is gone, test whether a residual audience exists: new queries in Trends, steady comments weeks later, repeat visitors.
5. Do not build a long product on a spike without a second signal from outside the wave.
Decision: window under two weeks means pre-sale or no-go; evergreen need behind the wave means validate with the 48-hour sprint (`playbooks.md` section 1).

## Report format
Evidence used and its rank on the ladder; most likely and second cause; the next smallest test with threshold and date; what could not be verified.

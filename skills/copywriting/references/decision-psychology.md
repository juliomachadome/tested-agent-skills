# Decision psychology: what to use and with what confidence

## Headlines and reading
- **[M] The headline carries the piece.** A 1960s print-ad estimate put five headline readers for each body reader; the web confirms the direction (people read ~20% of a page). Headline + subhead + button must sell on their own. (Ogilvy 1963)
- **[M] Headline strength order: self-interest, then news, then curiosity.** Curiosity alone loses to a clear benefit. (Caples, *Tested Advertising Methods*, 1932)
- **[M] Slippery slide:** the headline exists to get the first sentence read, the first sentence the second. Short first sentence; each block ends with a question the next answers. (Sugarman 2006)
- **[M] Useful testimonials are specific and attributable:** situation before, what they did, concrete result, name and context. Vague praise moves nothing and looks invented.

## Two modes of thinking
- **[M] The fast mode decides whether to keep reading** (headline, image, hierarchy); the slow mode audits price, proof and guarantee; the piece must pass both. Do not cite *Thinking, Fast and Slow* as proof of everything: its priming chapter relied on studies that failed to replicate, which the author acknowledged. (Kahneman 2011; Schimmack et al. 2017)
- **[M] Loss aversion exists but is ~2x, not 2.5x.** Meta-analysis of 607 estimates in 150 papers: mean coefficient 1.955 (95% CI 1.82-2.10). Serious critics say it depends on context and fades for small stakes. Use it to show the real cost of not acting; never to invent a loss. (Brown, Imai, Vieider & Camerer, JEL 2024; Gal & Rucker 2018)
- **[C] Fluency:** easy-to-read text seems truer and its author more competent; needless difficult words reduce credibility. (Oppenheimer 2006)

## Influence principles with their ethical line
(Cialdini, *Influence*, new and expanded ed., 2021)

| Principle | Legitimate use | Becomes manipulation when |
|---|---|---|
| Reciprocity | give something genuinely useful first (sample, diagnosis) | "free" hides a cost or forced signup |
| Commitment and consistency | small voluntary steps (quiz, chosen goal) | the answer is used to shame ("you said you wanted…") |
| Social proof | real numbers and testimonials from people like the reader | invented numbers, random "X just bought" |
| Authority | verifiable credentials, cited sources | fictional persona presented as an expert |
| Liking | the reader's language, a brand with a human face | fake intimacy, guilt |
| Scarcity | limits that exist (real date, real seats) | resetting timer, fake stock of a digital product |
| Unity | real shared identity ("for shift workers") | manufactured "us vs them" |

- **[M] Social proof works best from peers and near the decision.** The classic hotel-towel study had mixed replication: treat as direction. (Goldstein, Cialdini & Griskevicius 2008; Bohner & Schlüter 2014)
- **[M] Choice overload is contextual:** a 50-experiment meta-analysis found a mean effect near zero. Keep the practical rule: one main action per screen. (Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd 2010)

## Myths and numbers not to repeat
| Claim | Status | Say instead |
|---|---|---|
| "Human attention is 8 seconds, less than a goldfish" | [X] no study behind it | people scan and read ~20% |
| "Losses hurt 2.5x more than gains" | [M] measured ~1.96 | "losses weigh about twice, depending on context" |
| "First-person buttons convert 90% more" | [F] one test | test it |
| "Long copy always sells" / "nobody reads long copy" | [X] both | length by awareness and objections |
| "More options always lower conversion" | [M] mean effect ~0 | one main action per screen |
| "There is a best formula (AIDA, PAS…)" | [F] | formulas organise, they do not prove |
| "The ideal subject line has N characters" | [F] | fact first, fits on a phone |
| "Mental triggers always work" | [X] fake triggers are illegal in the EU and raise refunds | only true triggers |
| "One word primes behaviour" | [X]/[F] did not replicate | do not base decisions on it |
| "Good readability score = clear text" | [X] measures surface | score + reading by people |

## What the Cialdini chapters add for a page (owner's copy: Portuguese edition, ch. 2, 3, 4 and 7)
Read against the table above. The studies are mostly 1970s-2000s lab and field work, often with students; later meta-analyses give smaller effects than the originals, so treat each item as a hypothesis to test, not a promise. Ethical line and EU limits stay as in the table and in `eu-consumer-law.md`.
- **Reciprocity, ch. 2 [M].** A gift given first and without strings moves people even toward someone they dislike, so the asset must be useful on its own and not gated behind the ask. Field examples in the book: money enclosed with a survey beat a bigger payment promised after, and a second small treat raised restaurant tips far more than one. On a page: put the free deliverable (checklist, first lesson, diagnosis) before the request, and say plainly what it costs the reader (nothing). The book's own warning: once people learn to recognise the tactic they avoid it, so a gimmick burns trust for every later page.
- **Reject-then-retreat (door in the face), ch. 2 [M].** A large request followed by a real, smaller one raised compliance from about 1 in 6 to 1 in 2 in the original study; an opening ask that looks absurd fails because the retreat no longer reads as a concession. Later meta-analyses find a small effect (see `offers`, `bump-upsell-downsell.md`). On a pricing page it is honest only if the bigger plan is really sold and the smaller one is a genuine alternative.
- **Commitment, ch. 3 [M].** Small, voluntary, active steps change how people see themselves and make a later, larger step easier (foot in the door; a tiny sign request lifted agreement to a much bigger one in the classic field study). Commitments that people write or declare themselves hold better than ones the seller writes: quizzes, a goal the reader types, an "I want to..." choice. Reflect their own words back; never use them to shame. Extracting a commitment and then adding a cost (low-ball, drip pricing) is a dark pattern; see `persuasion.md` section 3.
- **Social proof, ch. 4 [M].** It works most when the reader is unsure what to do and when the people shown are like the reader (a fundraiser who said "I'm a student here too" more than doubled gifts). Place proof where doubt peaks (price, first use of something unfamiliar) and pick examples from the reader's segment. Seeding a new behaviour with visible early users is legitimate only if they are real users.
- **Scarcity, ch. 7 [M].** Scarcity raises wanting, not judged quality: in the book's biscuit study the scarce ones were wanted more but not rated tastier, so a real limit never replaces proof of value. Loss framing helps most when the choice is uncertain or risky. Restricting access raises interest (reactance), and rivalry over a scarce item makes people overpay; avoid auction-style countdowns. The book's door-to-door "I cannot come back" line exists to stop the buyer from thinking; if the limit is invented it is a banned practice in the EU (`eu-consumer-law.md`).

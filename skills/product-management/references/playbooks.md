# Playbooks: routines for product work

Evidence tags: **[C]** consensus, **[M]** serious but single, old or practitioner-derived, **[F]** vendor or opinion, **[X]** myth. Ideas are applied, never copied; sources in `BIBLIOGRAPHY.md`. Symptoms that send you here: `diagnosis-playbook.md`. Metric and experiment depth: `skills/growth-analytics`. Screens and flows: `skills/ux-design`.

## 1. Discovery sprint (one week, 5-8 interviews)
**Day 0 - frame.** Write the desired outcome (one metric, a direction, a segment), the riskiest belief, and who you can reach this week. Recruit 5-8 people in one segment who did the job recently; not friends, not only fans. Get consent to record; say what you will do with the data; keep personal data minimal and delete on schedule.
**Days 1-3 - interviews (30-45 min).** Two people: one asks, one notes verbatim.
Script (past behaviour, not opinions):
1. Warm-up: role, context, one line on what they do.
2. Last time: "Tell me about the last time you [did the job]." Let them tell the story; follow with "then what?".
3. Trigger: "What happened that made you look for a way to do this?"
4. Alternatives: what they used, what it cost in money, time, nerves, and why that one.
5. Struggle: where it went wrong, what they did next, who else was involved.
6. Commitment: "Would you try X now / introduce me to Y / pay for a pilot?" Compliments are noise; time, money, introductions and data are signal **[M]**.
7. Close: "Who else should I talk to? What did I not ask?"
Mom Test rules (Fitzpatrick) **[M]**: talk about their life, not your idea; ask about specifics in the past, not generics or hypotheticals ("would you" invites polite lies); deflect compliments and ask what they did; never pitch before the story is told; always leave with a next step or a clear no; write down facts, not your interpretation.
**Day 4 - synthesise.** One interview, one snapshot: context, trigger, struggle, alternatives, quotes, surprises. Cluster needs, pains and desires across snapshots into opportunities, phrased from the customer's side ("I can't tell which supplier is late"), not as solutions. Count how many people mentioned each (n of N), and mark the strength of the evidence.
**Day 5 - tree.** Build the opportunity solution tree (Torres) **[M]**: outcome at the top; opportunities beneath (parent, child); at least three candidate solutions for the chosen opportunity; one assumption test per solution. Pick one target opportunity, not a basket; compare solutions side by side. Output: tree outline, top three opportunities with evidence counts, the riskiest assumptions, next tests, what you could not ask. Rule of thumb: stop interviewing a segment when the last 2-3 interviews give no new opportunity, not at a fixed number. Keep a weekly touchpoint with customers after the sprint.
**JTBD switch interview** (for a product people recently adopted or left): see `discovery-and-jtbd.md`.

## 2. Assumption mapping and cheap tests
**Map.** List assumptions behind the chosen solution in four types: desirability (they want it), usability (they can use it), feasibility (we can build it), viability (it works for the business and the law). Place each on a 2x2: how important to success vs how much evidence exists. Test first the top-left quadrant: important and unproven. Write for each test: the assumption, the method, the metric, the threshold that decides pass or fail, and the date, before running it.
**Methods, cheapest first** **[M]**:
| Method | What it tests | Example threshold | Ethics note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Interview or survey on past behaviour | desirability of the problem | at least 5 of 8 report the problem in the last month | consent; no leading questions |
| Data pull | size, frequency | at least 10% of active users do the workaround weekly | privacy law; aggregate |
| Fake door (a button or page for a feature that does not exist yet) | intent to act | 5% of those shown click; then follow-up | Say it is coming and not available as soon as they click; offer a waitlist or a call; no payment is taken; remove after the test; never for safety, health or money-critical features |
| Concierge (you do the service by hand for a few customers) | whether the outcome is valued and how the job really goes | 3 of 5 ask to continue | The customer knows a person is doing it; do not claim automation; protect their data like production |
| Wizard of Oz (the interface looks automated, a person behind it) | usability and value of the experience | task completion and repeat use | Tell users that a person may review their input if they share personal data; check consent and law; do not use it for decisions about people (credit, health, employment) |
| Prototype test (clickable) | usability | 4 of 5 complete without help | with ux-design |
| Smoke test (landing page with real offer, then honest "not yet") | demand | cost per sign-up against a limit | no fake scarcity or fake testimonials; EU consumer law applies |
| Paid pilot or pre-order | willingness to pay | 3 paid pilots at a stated price | real refund path and delivery |
Rules: the test must be able to fail; one assumption per test; sample-size caution (5 users find usability problems, not rates); do not sell something you cannot deliver. Experiments with live traffic: design and reading in growth-analytics.

## 2b. Validated learning, MVP, innovation accounting and pivots (Ries) [M]
Evidence: The Lean Startup is a practitioner synthesis built on the author's startups and stories (IMVU, Dropbox, Zappos and others); the cases are survivors and show a method, not proof it works better than alternatives. Ries himself says to apply the principles, not fixed tactics (Ries, The Lean Startup, ch. 1-8).
- **Validated learning (introduction, ch. 7).** Progress is what you learned about customers with evidence, not what you shipped. Write the leap-of-faith assumptions behind the plan; the two that sit above the rest are the **value hypothesis** (the product creates value for real customers) and the **growth hypothesis** (new customers will arrive and keep coming) (ch. 5). Test the riskiest first, in the order that could kill the plan.
- **MVP is the fastest loop through build, measure, learn with the least effort, not the smallest product** (ch. 6). Its purpose is to test a business hypothesis, not a technical or design question. Forms, cheapest first: a **video** or demo page that shows how it would work and measures sign-ups (Dropbox) **[F]**; a **concierge** MVP where people do the service by hand for a few customers, which tests value and teaches the real workflow (Food on the Table); a front that looks automated with people behind it (see the Wizard of Oz row in section 2, with its disclosure rules); a landing page or pre-order. A rule for scope: remove every feature, process or step that does not contribute directly to the learning you want (ch. 6). Quality rule: if you do not know who the customer is, you do not know what quality means; low quality may show which attributes they care about, but a defect that blocks learning is waste.
- **State the number before you run it.** "We assume 10% of visitors who see the offer will start a trial": that sentence is the test. Ries calls getting the first real numbers the **baseline** (ch. 7).
- **Innovation accounting in three steps (ch. 7).** (1) Baseline from an MVP: conversion, activation, retention and willingness to pay as they are, even if bad. (2) Tune the engine: each change is a hypothesis aimed at one driver of the growth model; a design that does not move customer behaviour has not worked, whatever the team feels. (3) Pivot or persevere when tuning stops moving the numbers toward the model. Compare two teams: one with a baseline, a hypothesis and an experiment; one that ships several changes and celebrates any number that rose.
- **Metrics that teach (ch. 7).** Prefer cohort reports (of people who joined in this period, how many did each behaviour) to running totals; totals show the rosiest picture and hide cohorts that are not improving (the IMVU example). Three tests for a metric: **actionable** (cause and effect are clear), **accessible** (anyone on the team understands it in people, not abstract counts), **auditable** (you can check it against real customers and the raw data). Split tests need a pre-stated hypothesis (growth analytics: sample size, peeking).
- **Pivot or persevere (ch. 8).** Warning signs: experiments get less productive over time; the team keeps adding features without behaviour change; nothing is clearly failing, so nothing is clearly learned (the living-dead startup). Hold a scheduled pivot-or-persevere meeting, bring cohort data and customer evidence, and agree beforehand what result would count as failure (a 'commitment to iterate'). A pivot is a structured change to test a new fundamental hypothesis, not any change. Catalogue: **zoom-in** (one feature becomes the product), **zoom-out** (the product becomes one feature), **customer segment** (same product, different customer), **customer need** (same customer, a different, more important problem), **platform** (app to platform or back), **business architecture** (high margin, low volume vs low margin, high volume), **value capture** (how you earn), **engine of growth** (sticky, viral or paid), **channel** (for example complex direct sales to self-serve) and **technology**. After a pivot, set a new baseline; a good pivot makes the tuning faster.
- **Engines of growth (ch. 10).** Sticky or recurring (new customers outrun churn), viral (a viral coefficient above 1: each new customer brings more than one), paid (the margin from each customer exceeds the cost of acquiring one and funds the next). Choose the one your model relies on and measure its single key ratio.
- **Cautions.** An MVP can mislead in one case: customers do not understand a new category from a description (the Dropbox video was for an audience that already knew the problem), so pair it with problem interviews (`discovery-and-jtbd.md`). Do not run an MVP where a failure harms people (health, money, safety) without a controlled, consented design, and do not claim automation that does not exist. A "false negative" from an MVP that is too thin is real: ask why they declined before pivoting.

## 3. PRD or one-pager
Keep `prd-template.md` as the skeleton; this is how to fill it.
- **Order of writing:** problem and evidence first, then metric and target, then non-goals, then the solution. A PRD that starts with the solution is a spec of a guess.
- **Problem:** two or three sentences; the person, the situation, what they do today, the cost; link evidence (interviews, tickets, data) and mark its strength.
- **Goal:** one primary metric with baseline, target and date, plus 1-2 guardrails (what must not get worse). If you cannot give a baseline, the first deliverable is the instrumentation.
- **Non-goals:** at least three, each the thing a reader might assume is included.
- **Solution:** flow in six steps or fewer; states (empty, loading, error, permission, offline); edge cases; owner of copy; accessibility notes. Interface detail goes to ux-design.
- **Acceptance criteria:** numbered, each one observable. "Given a signed-in member with no items, when they open the list, then an empty state with one primary action is shown." Add error and permission cases; avoid words like "fast" without a number (say 95% of requests under 400 ms and how it is measured).
- **Risks and assumptions:** each with a test and an owner; the riskiest first (section 2).
- **Rollout:** flag, cohort, monitoring, the rollback trigger as a number ("error rate above 2% for 15 minutes").
- **Instrumentation:** event names and properties needed to read the goal; tracking plan in growth-analytics.
- **Open questions:** each with an owner and a deadline.
Working-backwards variant (Amazon) **[F]**: write the customer announcement and a FAQ first; if the announcement is dull or the FAQ has no answer to "why would they switch", the idea is not ready. Single-company practice, not tested outside it. Review: one reader per role (engineering, design, support, legal when relevant) answers "what is unclear?"; ask them to state the problem and metric aloud. Version it; record decisions in section 8.

## 4. Prioritisation session (60-90 minutes)
Before: outcome agreed; 10-20 candidates, each with a one-line problem, evidence link and effort estimate from the people who build it. Include "do nothing" and "buy or reuse" as rows.
**Choosing the tool:**
| Tool | Use when | Skip when |
|---|---|---|
| RICE (reach x impact x confidence / effort) | many similar-size items competing for one outcome | items differ hugely in size or reach cannot be estimated |
| ICE (impact, confidence, ease, each 1-10, averaged) | quick triage, growth ideas, tiny teams | you need to compare across teams; scores are subjective |
| Kano (survey with a functional and a dysfunctional question per feature) | deciding what is table stakes, performance or delighter in a mature product | pre-launch with no users; delighters decay into expected over time |
| Cost of delay / WSJF (relative cost of delay divided by relative job size; cost of delay = value, time criticality, risk reduction or opportunity enablement) | deadlines, seasonal windows, regulation, dependencies | no believable cost per week; the numbers are invented to win |
| Impact/effort grid | first filter in a workshop | final ranking |
Cost of delay logic (Reinertsen) **[M]**: delay has a cost per week; when jobs differ in size, do the highest cost-of-delay-per-week-of-work first. Example: A costs 10,000 per week of delay and needs 2 weeks (5,000 per week of work); B costs 6,000 per week of delay and needs 1 week (6,000); B first, then A, even though A looks bigger.
**Run it:** (1) restate the outcome and constraints; (2) each estimator scores alone first, then compare to expose disagreement; (3) discuss the biggest gaps, not the averages; (4) range scores for reach and effort and use the low case for confidence; (5) sanity checks: does the top item serve the outcome, who estimated effort, what did we assume about adoption, what is the smallest slice that teaches us; (6) produce: ordered list, "not now" list with reasons, owner per item, next review date. Technical debt and reliability compete in the same list as risk or capacity cost. No formula decides **[C]**; it organises the argument.

## 5. Outcome roadmap (Now / Next / Later)
Table columns: horizon, outcome sought (metric and direction), opportunity or problem, evidence and its strength, owner, confidence (high, medium, low), what would make us stop. **Now** (this cycle): committed scope with a metric; **Next** (1-2 cycles): problems being discovered, solutions open; **Later**: themes with low confidence, no dates. Dates only for external commitments (law, contract, event) and label them. Review monthly and after each learning; publish what changed and why in a short note. A roadmap promised by date to customers is a contract; do not use this format for contracts. Test: remove the dates and the items; can a new team member still say what you are trying to change?

## 6. Launch checklist and post-launch review
Before launch: acceptance criteria passed (QA); metric, baseline, target and kill-or-scale rule written; events firing and checked in production; rollout plan (flag, cohort, steps, rollback trigger); support has docs, known issues and an escalation path; legal and privacy checked where relevant; analytics consent respected; communication (in-product, email, changelog) with claims you can prove; owner on call; a review date booked.
**30 days:** adoption funnel (aware, tried, repeated) on eligible users; defects and support themes; guardrails; interview 5 users. Decision: fix, promote or hold.
**60 days:** repeat use and retention of adopters vs a comparison group; effect on the outcome metric where an experiment or holdout allows; cost to run. Decision: invest, keep, or plan removal.
**90 days:** outcome result against target; was the assumption right; what did we learn; what do we remove. Write three lines in the decision log. A feature that missed its stated threshold gets a removal plan, not another quarter by default **[M]**. Diagnosis of low results: `diagnosis-playbook.md`.

## 7. Metrics tree and North Star
Start from the value delivered to the customer and the business. North Star (one metric that reflects delivered value and predicts revenue, for example weekly active teams that completed the core action) **[F]**; break into 3-5 drivers (reach, activation, frequency, depth, retention); each into inputs a team can move this quarter. For each node: definition, owner, source, guardrail, and whether it is leading or lagging. Avoid vanity totals (cumulative sign-ups) and ratios without denominators. A North Star that rises while retention falls is wrong. Do not set it before you know the job. Definitions, tracking plans, experiments and cohorts: `skills/growth-analytics`. Product-market-fit survey (the share who would be "very disappointed" without the product; 40 percent is a practitioner rule of thumb from one dataset) **[F]**, sample only active users and report n.

## 8. Saying no, and recording decisions
Formula: (1) restate their goal; (2) the evidence you have and lack; (3) the trade-off in numbers or in what we drop; (4) the alternative or the cheap test; (5) what evidence would change your mind, and when you will look again. Example: "The goal is to win accounts like this one. 2 of 31 interviews asked for it; building costs 3 person-months, which pushes out the onboarding fix worth about 1,600 on our scale. I suggest a fake door this week; if 5% of those shown click, I will put it in Next." For a senior stakeholder, bring the options and the cost of each, and ask them to choose with the numbers, not to argue with the score. Never hide a decision you lost: record it and the review date. Cutting scope mid-project follows the same form.
**Decision record** (adapted from Nygard's architecture decision records **[F]**): title; context (pressures, evidence); options; decision ("we will ..."); status (proposed, accepted, superseded by); consequences (good, bad, neutral); review date. Keep it in the repository next to the PRD.

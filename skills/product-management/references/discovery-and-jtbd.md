# Discovery and jobs to be done

Routines (sprint, assumption tests, synthesis) are in `playbooks.md` 1-2; symptoms in `diagnosis-playbook.md`. Evidence tags as in the SKILL: **[M]** serious but single or practitioner-derived, **[F]** vendor or opinion.

## Interview guide (past behaviour)
1. Context: tell me about the last time you [did the job].
2. Trigger: what happened that made you look for a solution then?
3. Alternatives: what did you try or use? Why that? What did it cost (money, time, nerves)?
4. Struggle: where did it go wrong or take longer than expected?
5. Commitment: what did you do next? Would you introduce me to someone, pay, or give your data to try this now? (Compliments are noise; commitments are signal.)
6. Close: who else should I speak to?
Avoid: "would you use...", "do you like...", "how much would you pay...", feature lists, and asking people to design the product.

## JTBD in one paragraph **[M]**
People "hire" a product to make progress in a situation. The job is the progress, not the product category; competitors are whatever else gets hired, including doing nothing or a spreadsheet. A job statement: When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]. Keep it free of the solution. Jobs have functional, emotional and social parts; interviews find them, surveys do not. JTBD is a lens for discovery, not a prioritisation formula; the evidence is case-based, not controlled.

## Switch interview (recent switchers, 30-60 min)
Interview people who switched to (or away from) a product in the last 1-3 months, while they still remember. Reconstruct the timeline:
1. **First thought**: when did you first think the old way was not good enough? What was going on that day?
2. **Passive looking**: what did you notice, read or hear, without searching? What did you not do yet and why?
3. **Active looking**: what did you compare? Which alternatives, who did you ask, what mattered?
4. **Deciding**: what almost stopped you? What tipped it? Who else was involved?
5. **First use**: what happened the first week? Did it do the job? Surprise?
6. **Ongoing**: what do you do now that you did not before? What do you still do the old way?
Ask for dates, places, people and quotes; probe with "what else was going on?" Do not ask why in the abstract; reconstruct the day.

## Forces of progress
- **Push** of the situation (what is wrong with the current way) and **pull** of the new solution (what it promises) drive a switch.
- **Anxiety** about the new (will it work, will I look foolish, data safety) and **habit** of the present (it is familiar, others rely on it) hold it back.
- A switch happens when push plus pull exceed anxiety plus habit. Map the four per interview, then count across interviews.
Decisions each force produces: push -> who to target and when to show up (the trigger); pull -> the promise and the first-use outcome; anxiety -> trial, migration help, guarantees, proof you can document, clear data handling; habit -> import, familiar patterns, a path that does not require stopping the old way on day one. Note: do not invent anxiety or urgency to lift conversion; fix the cause.

## Reading across interviews
Write each interview as a one-page snapshot (situation, trigger, struggle, alternatives, forces, quotes, surprises). Cluster into opportunities phrased from the customer's side and count n of N. Observation (what they did) stays separate from interpretation (what we think). Build the opportunity solution tree from the clusters (`playbooks.md` 1). Interviews tell why, surveys and analytics tell how many **[C]**; size a promising opportunity afterwards with a survey or a data pull.

## Assumption testing
List assumptions on desirability, usability, feasibility, viability; rank by importance and missing evidence; test the riskiest cheaply (fake door, prototype, concierge, one-question survey, data pull) with a pass threshold stated beforehand. Methods and ethics: `playbooks.md` 2.

## Evidence strength (low to high)
Opinion < anecdote < repeated interview pattern < observed behaviour < paid commitment < controlled experiment. Say which level supports a claim.

## Interview rules in depth (Fitzpatrick, The Mom Test, ch. 1-8) [M]
Evidence: one practitioner's field book; no controlled comparison of interview styles. Treat the rules as a checklist that removes common bias, not as proof.
- **Three rules (ch. 1):** talk about their life, not your idea; ask about specific past events, not opinions about the future; talk less and listen more. A question is good if even a loving relative could not give you a false positive.
- **Three kinds of bad data (ch. 2):** compliments, fluff and unprompted ideas. *Compliments* ("great idea") cost the speaker nothing and mean nothing; deflect them and return to facts. *Fluff* has three shapes: generic claims ("I usually", "I always"), future promises ("I would buy", "I will use") and hypotheticals ("I might"). Anchor fluff to the last time it happened: "When was the last time? What did you do? What else did you try?" Question stems that invite fluff: "would you...", "do you ever...", "could you imagine...". *Ideas and feature requests* are clues, not specs: ask why they want it, what it would let them do, how they cope today, what it costs them. Explore strong emotions (anger, embarrassment, delight): the reason is often the real driver (a customer asked for report logos only to look good to their own clients).
- **Do not give people a reason to protect you (ch. 2).** If your ego or an excited pitch shows, people soften and praise. If you catch yourself pitching, apologise briefly and return to what they were describing. Do not interrupt to correct their reading of your idea; the misunderstanding is data.
- **Importance probes (ch. 1, 3):** "How are you coping now? What did it cost last time? Have you looked for a fix, paid for one, built a workaround? Where does the money come from, who else decides?" A problem nobody has tried to fix is not one they will pay to fix. Start with their goals and priorities before zooming into your problem: if the problem is not in their top few, details about it are noise. Include one question that could kill your idea in each conversation and welcome a flat "meh": indifference is more informative than praise.
- **Commitment and advancement (ch. 5).** After you have something to show, every conversation ends in a commitment or a clear no; "it went well" does not count. Currencies: **time** (a next meeting with an agenda, a trial used for a stated period, a prototype review), **reputation** (an introduction to the boss or the team, a public reference or case study), **money** (letter of intent, pre-order, deposit, paid pilot). Stronger commitments combine currencies. Vague closings ("looks great, tell me when it launches", "I know two people I could introduce") are polite exits; ask for something concrete, today. Leads that keep chatting but never commit are **zombie leads**: ask for the next concrete step or a no. Early customers are emotional: the problem is theirs, they know it, they have a budget, and have tried a workaround. Do not mistake enthusiasm of casual contacts for them.
- **Segment (ch. 7).** If feedback contradicts itself, the segment is too wide (you are having one conversation each with several different customers). Slice until you can name a "who and where" you can reach this week. In B2B, talk to every role (user, buyer, budget owner, blocker); do not talk only to the most senior people. A marketplace needs a separate conversation per side.
- **Process (ch. 8).** Before: write the 3 biggest unknowns and the commitment you will ask for; 5 minutes of desk research on the person and company. During: two people at most, one asks and one notes verbatim. After: share notes with the whole team the same day, update the answers to the 3 questions; do not let one person become the "dictator of facts" who says what customers want. If you cannot say what you want to learn, do not hold the call.
- **Where it stops working:** when people do not know they have the problem or cannot picture the category, life-based questions give little; a demo or concierge test is needed (`playbooks.md` 2 and 2b). Asking for a commitment too early can press people into false precision; ask for the smallest real one. The book suggests inventing a cover story to get a meeting; do not: say you are researching a problem, and if you later want to sell, say so (honest consent, `SKILL.md` 10).

## Customer discovery as a method (Blank and Dorf, The Startup Owner's Manual, ch. 3-6) [F]
Evidence: practitioner method from the authors' teaching and companies; used by many teams, with no controlled trials; thresholds are rules of thumb.
- **Four phases (ch. 3-6):** (1) write your business-model hypotheses (the canvas below); (2) test the problem with a one-page problem presentation, not a product demo; (3) test the solution with a low-fidelity MVP (a mock-up, a video, a prototype) and then a higher-fidelity one; (4) verify the model and pivot or proceed. Customers and prospects are met outside the building, not by survey alone.
- **Problem meeting (ch. 4):** the page lists your assumed problems, how people solve them today, and your idea in one line; at least half the pauses ask for more than yes or no. Ask them to rank the problems, mark must-solve vs nice-to-solve, say what the problem costs (lost revenue, lost customers, time), which three problems they would fix this year, how they evaluate new products, and who else shares the problem. Close by asking what hurts most in how they work, what you should have asked, and which three other well-informed people to talk to. Take the top three things you need to learn into each call. Never leave a conversation, even a bad one, without three new facts.
- **Score the data (ch. 4):** a scorecard per prospect: excited, urgent need, business impact, uses a workaround, likelihood to buy within a stated time, spoke to someone who can decide. Weight by what matters to the company's runway; keep outlier comments. If many say "urgent" and few say "this quarter", suspect a weak value or price story, or the wrong people.
- **Verify before scaling (ch. 6):** did most prospects rate the problem 8-10 out of 10; did many have a workaround; did the MVP draw strong "buy" or "engage" reactions and referrals; is the before/after workflow clearly different; do prospects say they would pay for the difference; are the numbers of customers and market size credible for the channel (rough guides in `founder-and-self-serve` section 7 of the sales skill); can you say who the customers are and how to reach them. A "no" on any is a pivot or iteration, not a reason to skip the step.
- **Sales side:** early customers, test selling, sales roadmap: `skills/sales/references/founder-and-self-serve.md` 7.

## Problem framing: Levine and the business-model canvas
**Fall in love with the problem (Levine, ch. 1, 7, 8) [F].** Evidence: a founder's memoir with his own startups (Waze, Moovit and others); survivorship and no controlled data; read as a set of prompts.
- Start from a frustration, then check that many people share it ("sample of one": you are one user; hear it from several people in different words before trusting it). Size a problem on two axes: how many people or companies have it, and how painful it is, by intensity and by frequency. Frequent problems (used daily) give more chances to build habit and word of mouth; a small audience with very high value suits a niche business; few people with a mild, rare problem are a poor bet.
- Tell the story from the user's side ("we help people avoid X") instead of "our system does Y"; it forces you to keep the problem in view.
- Problems can disappear or shrink after a competitor or an institution fixes them (his parcel-pickup startup lost its need when the post office changed its service); check the problem again on a calendar, not just once.
- Find the felt value: what he thought was time saved in a navigation app was largely peace of mind; ask people what they get from the outcome, not only the task.
- **Product-market fit is measured by return, not by launch (ch. 8):** consumer: cohort retention; B2B: renewal and expansion by the same customer; plus conversion (share of new users who reach the core value). Users who joined for the story but did not come back mean you have not found it. The way to the first number: walk the funnel of a first-time user, find the three kinds of barrier (understanding what it does, reaching the value, deciding the value is enough), measure each drop, then **talk to the people who failed there** (retained users no longer see the barrier and the team cannot see it either). Remedies per barrier: remove it or move it later (for example ask for registration after value), simplify, shorten the copy, direct attention with visual hierarchy. Observe new users silently using the product, then ask why.
- **Early adopters differ from the early majority (ch. 7):** adopters tolerate rough edges and explore; the majority wait for proof, ask for references and quit sooner. Simplicity (fewer features, fewer steps) is the bridge. Builders are rarely typical users; observe a range.
**The canvas as a framing tool (Osterwalder and Pigneur, Business Model Generation, 2010; the canvas pp. 14-55, customer insights and prototyping pp. 126-179) [F].** Evidence: a practitioner handbook co-created by many practitioners; it describes a model, it does not validate one, and no controlled study shows it improves results.
- Nine blocks: customer segments, value propositions, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partners, cost structure. Use it to write each belief as a testable hypothesis, starting with one segment and one problem (an unfocused canvas hides which segment the value proposition is for).
- Value-proposition prompts to ask, per segment, which one the person's job really needs: newness, performance, customisation, getting the job done, design, brand or status, price, cost reduction, risk reduction, accessibility, convenience or usability. Then check by interview which of these they have paid for or worked around.
- Empathy map for one named customer: what they see, hear, say and do, think and feel, their pains and gains. Draft it as hypotheses, then replace each line with interview evidence; never present the draft as research.
- Prototype the model in steps: a napkin sketch, a full canvas, a spreadsheet of revenue and costs under different assumptions, then a field test (the book treats prototyping as a way to explore many models before committing, pp. 160-169).
Sources: Christensen et al., Moesta and Engle, Torres, Fitzpatrick, Blank and Dorf, Ries, Levine, Osterwalder and Pigneur (`BIBLIOGRAPHY.md`).

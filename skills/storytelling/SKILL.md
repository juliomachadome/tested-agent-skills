# Storytelling

A story is a character who wants something, meets an obstacle, and is changed by how it resolves. Without a want there is no direction; without an obstacle no tension; without change no point. Everything below serves those three things for one audience in one medium. Evidence tags: **[C]** consensus of craft or replicated research, **[M]** serious but single, old or correlational, **[F]** practitioner convention, **[X]** myth. Ideas are restated, never copied.

## 1. Start: project first, then Step 0
Read the current project's docs first (brand voice, audience research, series bible, past scripts, legal and disclosure rules); project rules override this skill. Then answer in five lines (details in `references/diagnosis-playbook.md`, Step 0): goal, audience, medium, length, and what the audience should feel or do at the end. If missing, ask for the minimum or state the assumption. A story with no job is decoration.

## 2. The minimum story
- **Character** the audience can picture: a person, not "users" or "the market" [F].
- **Want** that can be stated in one sentence and tested by the end ("get the loan before Friday") [C].
- **Obstacle** that makes the want costly: an opponent, a flaw, a deadline, a choice between two goods [C].
- **Change**: the character, the situation or the audience's belief is different at the end [C].
- **Stakes**: what is lost if the want fails. Name them early, raise them as the story runs [F].
If one is missing, the story feels like an anecdote, a report or an ad. Fix the missing piece before polishing sentences.

## 3. Pick a structure (tools, not laws)
| Option | Use when | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Story spine (once upon a time, every day, until one day, because of that, until finally, ever since) | Short pieces, pitches, a first draft of anything; fast to test | Thin on character depth [F] |
| Three acts (setup, confrontation, resolution) | Films, episodes, talks of 3+ minutes | Can become a formula that hides a missing want [M] |
| Hero's journey (call, threshold, trials, ordeal, return with a gift) | Origin stories, transformation arcs, long series with a mentor and a mission | A comparative-myth model turned into a recipe; it assumes departure and return, a male-coded quest in its classic telling, and one central hero. It misleads for ensemble stories, slice of life, tragedy without return, and for short pieces where 12 stages are padding [M] |
| Beat sheet (Save the Cat style: opening image, catalyst, break into two, midpoint, all is lost, finale, final image) | Screenplay-length pacing checks and series planning | A practitioner heuristic from the film market, useful for timing, not a law of nature [F] |
Choose by length and medium (`references/variants/README.md`). Use one structure at a time; mixing three produces mush.

## 4. Character, desire and stakes
Give the lead a **want** (outer goal), a **need** (inner lesson the want hides) and a **flaw** that makes the obstacle hard. Stakes must be personal first, then larger: a clinic closing matters because of one nurse. Raise the cost of the want at least once (midpoint turn) [F]. `references/playbooks.md` has the character sheet.

## 5. Scenes and turns
A scene is a unit where someone wants something, meets resistance, and ends changed: a value flips (safe to unsafe, hope to doubt). If a scene ends where it began, cut or merge it [C]. Use reversals: the audience expects X, gets Y that is both surprising and inevitable. Enter late, leave early. Scene card in `playbooks.md`.

## 6. Open questions and payoff
Hold attention by raising a question (will she get it, who did this, what is in the box) and answering later, with smaller questions inside larger ones [C]. Every promise needs a payoff; every payoff needs setup. The ending must close the central question and show the change; an ending that only stops is flat. Series end each episode on a smaller payoff and an open larger question.

## 7. Specificity and voice
Specific beats general: the 6:40 train, not "a commute". Concrete detail (a number, a name, an object, a sensory fact) makes the character real and lets the audience project themselves [F]. Voice is consistent point of view and rhythm; cut hedges, abstractions and the narrator explaining the feeling the scene should create. Generic output (especially from AI) fails here first.

**Series note.** Episodes need a repeatable engine (same want-obstacle machine, new variation), stakes that rise across episodes, a short recap for new viewers, and a planned end; a season without an ending date drifts. See `references/variants/series-interactive.md`.

## AI tells to avoid (check while creating, not after)
- Stock openings ("In a world where...", "It was a day like any other").
- Tidy three-act beats with no specific detail; every scene ending on a reveal.
- Names like Elara or Kael; a cast with no particular history.
- Emotions stated ("she felt hopeless") instead of shown through action and objects.
- A moralising last paragraph that explains the lesson.
- Purple prose, stacked adjectives, inflated symbolism, vague "some say" attributions.
- Em-dash and rule-of-three habits, "not X but Y" parallelisms, filler.
- Fix: concrete sensory detail, a specific want with a cost, the creator's own voice. Say what you chose and why in one line (goal, audience, platform, budget, rights); a choice without a reason is removed.
- If a humanizer skill is installed, run it as a final pass on the finished text, then re-check facts and numbers it may have touched. Never depend on it.

## Quick test before any draft goes out
1. Whose story is it, and what do they want in one sentence? 2. What stops them? 3. What do they lose if they fail? 4. What is different at the end? 5. Which detail could only belong to this story? Two or more "I do not know" answers mean go back to section 2, not to wording. For a company, the customer is the hero and the company is the guide; the brand as hero reads as boasting [F].

## 8. Why stories work (and what not to claim)
Narrative transportation, being absorbed into a story, is associated with story-consistent beliefs and fewer counterarguments [C: Green and Brock 2000; Escalas 2007 on narrative versus analytic processing]. Identification with the character and curiosity about the open question carry attention. Popular claims that a single hormone or "neural coupling" explains persuasion are suggestive lab work, popularised beyond its scope [M]; do not build a promise on them. For why people stop, watch, share and follow short videos, use `skills/viral-content` (diagnosis by metric, `references/field-study-10-reels.md`); this skill is the craft behind the content, not the distribution.

## 9. Ethics
- No invented testimonials, customers, quotes or statistics presented as real. Composite or illustrative characters must be labelled as such [C, consumer law and platform rules].
- Disclose fiction when it could be mistaken for fact, and disclose AI-generated people, voices or scripts where law or platform rules require it (EU AI Act transparency, platform labels) [C]; check current rules.
- Real people in your story: accuracy, consent where private, no humiliation for engagement.
- Do not use stakes manipulatively: no fake urgency or invented tragedy to move a buyer.

## 11. Output
Deliver: the story in the requested form, the one-sentence logline (want, obstacle, stakes), what changed and why it works, what was assumed, and anything not verified (facts, rights, legal claims). Do not pad with all structures at once; name the one chosen and why. Hand off final sales copy to copywriting, ad specs to ad-creatives, and distribution to viral-content.

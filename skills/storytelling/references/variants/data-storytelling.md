# Data storytelling

Numbers do not speak; someone chooses what they mean. The narrative layer sits on top of correct analysis. Evidence tags as in `SKILL.md`.

## 1. One insight
Write the single sentence the audience should repeat ("Customers who miss the first week churn twice as fast"). If you have five insights, make five stories or pick one. The analysis (correct numbers, uncertainty, method) belongs to data-analysis; get that right first.

## 2. Context
Say who, when and against what: a number without a comparison is not a story. Include sample size or source where it affects trust. State what the data cannot show.

## 3. Contrast
The story is the gap: before and after, plan versus actual, us versus benchmark, segment A versus B. Show the contrast, then the person or case behind it (one real account, with permission), then the action.

## 4. Structure
Setup (what we expected), conflict (what the data showed), resolution (what to do). Order slides or sections accordingly; keep charts to the ones that carry the contrast, and focus attention with one highlighted element (the practitioner view in Knaflic's book on storytelling with data) [F].

## 5. Honesty
Do not choose the axis, time window or segment to force the story; do not present correlation as cause; show uncertainty. If the data does not support the narrative, change the narrative. An illustrative character must be labelled as illustrative.

## 6. Check
Can the audience say the insight, the contrast and the action after one pass? Run the revision checklist. Chart construction and statistics go to data-analysis.

## 7. Worked example
Raw finding: "Retention is 62% at day 30." No story. Add context and contrast: "Of 4,000 trial users, 62% were active at day 30, but only 38% of those who skipped the setup checklist were, versus 71% who completed it (counts and period stated). The gap is the story: setup is where users decide." Person: one anonymised user's path with permission. Action: move the checklist into the first screen and test it. Check that the comparison groups are comparable and say what you cannot conclude (people who finish setup may already be more motivated).

## 8. Common failures
- A chart tour with no insight: the audience leaves with nothing to repeat.
- The one number that surprises is hidden on slide 14.
- A story built from a single vivid case presented as typical. Say how typical it is.
- Too many highlighted elements: nothing stands out.
- Ending on "more research is needed" with no action: finish with one decision the audience can make this week.

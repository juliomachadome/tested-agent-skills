# Series and interactive stories

Episodic stories fail by drift, not by one bad scene. Plan the engine, the season and the continuity before episode 1. Evidence tags as in `SKILL.md`.

## 1. Premise testing
Write the premise as a logline (`../playbooks.md` 5). Test: (a) is there a repeatable engine, i.e. a want-obstacle machine that produces new episodes (a case of the week, a heist, a contest)? (b) can you list ten episode ideas in ten minutes? (c) does the lead have a flaw that guarantees conflict for the whole season? If (b) fails, the premise is a single story, not a series. Test it on five people from the intended audience: ask what they would watch next, not whether they like it.

## 2. Episode beats
Each episode is a complete small story plus a step in the season arc:
- Cold open with the question of the episode (first 3 to 10 seconds for short-form).
- The episode want and obstacle; a midpoint turn that raises the cost.
- A payoff to the episode question.
- A closing hook: one larger question left open (cliffhanger), or a personal cost the lead now carries.
Short-form episodes (60-120 s): use the scene card for 4-6 scenes; keep one location per scene and one prop that carries the plot.

## 3. Season arc and when to end
A season needs a central question announced early, midpoint escalation, a low point near 75-80% and a finale that pays it. Plan the number of episodes from the number of beats, not from the calendar. Stakes must rise: each episode costs more than the last, or the audience senses a treadmill [F]. End the series when the central want is resolved or the engine repeats without raising stakes; a planned ending beats a decline. If audience data exists (per-episode completion, return rate), use it to decide, otherwise treat the call as editorial judgment and say so.

## 4. Vote mechanics (interactive)
Audience votes give agency but can break the story. Rules that tend to hold [F]:
- Vote on choices that the lead faces, not on outcomes ("what does she do" not "does she win").
- Give two to three options, each with a real cost; avoid a safe option.
- Close the vote on a schedule and commit to the result; reveal that the vote shaped the story.
- Keep veto power: the writer protects the central question, tone and rights; do not let a vote run the plot into a contradiction.
- Disclose how votes are counted; do not fake a result or stage a vote that was not used. Check platform rules for contests and polls, and consent for any audience-submitted content.

## 5. Continuity bible
One file the whole production reads: characters (want, need, flaw, voice, appearance), locations, timeline, rules of the world, open questions and where each will pay, running motifs, and a "never again" list. For AI-generated visuals, include seed, prompt and reference images for each character to avoid drift. Update after every episode; check each new script against the open-questions list.

## 6. Recap and cliffhanger
- Recap: five seconds or two lines that state who wants what and what just changed; place it after the hook, not before. Without recap, new viewers leave in the first minute [F].
- Cliffhanger: raise a question the next episode answers within its first third; never use a cliffhanger that the next episode cheats (the threat was fake) because it erodes trust.

## 7. Link to distribution
Why people stop, watch, rewatch, share and follow is covered in `skills/viral-content` (including `references/field-study-10-reels.md`). Use it for retention reading and posting cadence; this file covers the story engine behind each episode.

## Worked example
A 12-episode micro-series: the lead wants to open a bakery before the lease ends on day 90. Engine: each episode a setback in the week (supplier, inspector, rival). Stakes rise: savings, then a friendship, then the family recipe. Low point: ep 9 loses the lease; ep 10 the vote decides whether she sells the recipe or asks the rival for help; ep 12 pays the day-90 question. Recaps are two lines each.

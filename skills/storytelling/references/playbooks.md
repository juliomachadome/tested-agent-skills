# Playbooks: templates and routines

Fill in, then run the revision checklist (section 9). Evidence tags as in `SKILL.md`.

## 1. Story spine
Fill one line per slot; it works as a first draft for almost anything short [F].
- Once upon a time ___ (character in a normal world).
- Every day ___ (routine; what they lack).
- Until one day ___ (the disruption; the want starts).
- Because of that ___ (consequence 1).
- Because of that ___ (consequence 2, stakes rise).
- Until finally ___ (the turn and the resolution).
- Ever since then ___ (the new normal: the change).

## 2. Three acts and a beat sheet
Percentages are guides for a feature-length shape; compress proportionally for shorter pieces [F].
- Act 1 (about 25%): opening image, setup, catalyst (the want is triggered), debate, break into act 2 (a choice).
- Act 2 (about 50%): fun and games (promise of the premise), midpoint (a win or loss that raises stakes), bad guys close in, all is lost, dark night.
- Act 3 (about 25%): break into act 3 (new insight), finale (the want faced with the lesson), final image (mirror of the opening to show change).
For 60 seconds: one beat per 8-10 seconds, usually setup, catalyst, two obstacles, low point, turn, payoff.

## 3. Character sheet
- Name and one physical detail. Role in the story.
- **Want** (outer, testable). **Need** (inner lesson). **Flaw** (what makes the obstacle hard).
- **Stakes**: what they lose; who else is affected.
- Voice: how they speak (length, slang, what they avoid).
- Secret or contradiction (one).
- Arc: from ___ to ___ (belief or behaviour).

## 4. Scene card
One card per scene.
- Where, when, who is present.
- Who wants what in this scene.
- What stops them.
- Value at start (e.g. trust) and at end (e.g. doubt).
- Turn (what changes mid-scene).
- Question raised, question answered.
- Detail only this scene has.
If start and end value match, cut or merge.

## 5. Logline
Formula: **When [inciting event] happens to [specific lead], they must [want] before [stakes/deadline], but [obstacle].** Example: When a night nurse finds her ward's funding was cut, she must keep it open until the audit on Monday, but the one doctor who can sign is her ex. Test: remove the lead's name and see if it still reads as a story; if the sentence could describe any hero, add specifics.

## 6. Pitch narrative (problem, stakes, solution, proof, ask)
1. **Problem** as a person's moment (one concrete case).
2. **Stakes**: what it costs them, with one true number and its source.
3. **Solution**: what changes, in plain words, as the turn.
4. **Proof**: a traction metric, a pilot result, a customer who agreed to be named; never an invented one.
5. **Ask**: one clear ask and the next step with a date.
Time budget for a 5-minute talk: 40% problem and stakes, 25% solution, 20% proof, 15% ask. Slide titles carry the story as sentences.

## 7. Brand story with the customer as hero
- Hero: the customer (named type, a real situation). Want, obstacle, what it costs them.
- Guide: the company (empathy: we have seen this; authority: proof it works).
- Plan: three steps. Call to action: one.
- Stakes: what failure looks like and what success looks like.
- Change: the customer's after state, in their words if real and consented.
Rule: if the first paragraph is about the company, rewrite the opening around the customer. Final sales copy then goes to copywriting.

## 8. Data story (one insight, context, contrast)
- One insight as a sentence the audience can repeat.
- Context: who, when, compared with what.
- Contrast: before and after, expected versus actual, us and the benchmark; the story is the gap.
- A person or a case behind the number.
- One action. Charts and statistics belong in data-analysis; here, shape the narrative around the single finding.

## 9. Revision checklist
- [ ] I can say the want, obstacle, stakes and change in four short sentences.
- [ ] The first question appears early for the medium.
- [ ] Every scene flips a value; none is a summary.
- [ ] Every setup is paid and every payoff is set up.
- [ ] Details are specific; no default names or generic adjectives.
- [ ] The ending closes the central question and shows the change without explaining the moral.
- [ ] No invented testimonials or statistics presented as real; fiction and AI use disclosed where needed.
- [ ] Facts and permissions verified; what was not verified is listed.
- [ ] Read aloud: no sentence you would not say.

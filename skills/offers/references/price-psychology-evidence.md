# Price psychology: what the evidence supports

## Setting the number
- **[C] Three rulers:** value (ceiling), alternatives (reference), cost and margin (floor). (Nagle & Müller, *The Strategy and Tactics of Pricing*)
- **[M] Stated willingness to pay overestimates:** a meta-analysis finds an average hypothetical bias around 21%. Van Westendorp and Gabor-Granger give a range, not an answer; real purchases decide. (Schmidt & Bijmolt, JAMS 2020)
- **[M] Price signals quality**, moderately, especially for products the buyer cannot judge before use: too cheap raises suspicion. (Rao & Monroe, JMR 1989 meta-analysis; Völckner & Hofmann 2007)
- **[F] In low ticket, small price changes move conversion little and margin directly** (practitioner report): test price early once volume exists.

## Anchoring and reference prices
- **[C] Anchoring in numeric judgement is robust** and replicated (Tversky & Kahneman 1974; Many Labs, Klein et al. 2014).
- **[M] Arbitrary anchors changing what people pay is shakier:** "coherent arbitrariness" replications found much smaller or null effects. Do not promise that a high number on the page doubles willingness to pay. (Ariely, Loewenstein & Prelec 2003; Maniadis, Tufano & List 2014)
- **[C] Advertised reference prices ("was X, now Y") raise perceived value and purchase intent, even when exaggerated**, which is exactly why the law regulates them. (Urbany, Bearden & Weilbaker 1988; Compeau & Grewal 1998; Ngwe 2018)
- **[M] Decoy and compromise effects work in the lab and often fail outside it;** the attraction effect fades when options are described realistically. Use a 2-3 plan menu only as an A/B test and only if every plan is something you would really sell. (Huber, Payne & Puto 1982; Frederick, Lee & Baskin 2014)
- **[M] Frequent discounts teach customers to wait** and raise price sensitivity long-term. (Kalyanaram & Winer 1995; Mela, Gupta & Lehmann 1997)
- **[M] Pennies-a-day framing** helps when computed from the real price (Gourville 1998). Recompute whenever the price changes.

## Charm and round prices
- **[M] Just-below prices (9.99, 97) help little.** Pre-registered meta-analysis (69 studies, 362 effects, ~40k participants): small effect on purchase (g = 0.13, CI 0.01-0.25), better price image (g = 0.28), no quality effect, prices more often underestimated (g = 0.67); bias correction suggests smaller, sometimes null effects. (Troll, Frankenbach, Friese & Loschelder, J. Consumer Psychology 2024)
- **[M] The mechanism is the left digit:** 2.99 reads as "two-something"; the effect appears when the left digit changes. (Thomas & Morwitz 2005)
- **[M] One field result:** the same dress sold more at 39 than at 34 or 44 in a catalogue test; US fashion retail, not proof for digital goods. (Anderson & Simester 2003)
- **[M] Round prices fit emotional or gift purchases; precise prices fit calculated ones.** (Wadhwa & Zhang 2015)
- **[M] Endings in 9 can signal "cheap/sale"** and hurt quality positioning. (Schindler & Kibarian 1996; Stiving 2000)
- **[M] "Free" is its own category:** demand jumps at zero more than the price drop explains. Valid for samples, not for items already in the price. (Shampanier, Mazar & Ariely 2007)
- **[F] Presentation tricks** (dropping the currency sign, smaller font) come from small context-specific studies: do not spend tests on them before testing offer and price.
- Practical rule: choose the right value band first, then adjust the ending just below a threshold the buyer notices (50, 100), respecting local habits (some markets end everything in 9, others use round prices).

## Guarantees and returns
- **[M] Lenient return policies raise purchases more than returns.** Meta-analysis of 21 papers: money and effort leniency raise purchases; scope leniency raises returns; longer windows tend to reduce returns. (Janakiraman, Syrdal & Freling, J. Retailing 2016)
- **[M] A money-back guarantee signals quality** when honoring it is costly for sellers of bad products; a guarantee full of conditions signals the opposite. (Moorthy & Srinivasan 1995; Suwelack et al. 2011)
- Types: unconditional, conditional ("if you do X and do not get Y"), anti-guarantee (final sale, justified). For cheap consumer products, the simple unconditional guarantee creates the least conflict, support and disputes. Conditional guarantees require proof of compliance and become fights. [F, practitioner framing]

## Bundles
- **[M] Weak bonuses lower the value of the whole** (Weaver, Garcia & Schwarz, "The Presenter's Paradox", JCR 2012). "Free" add-ons devalue themselves (Raghubir 2004; Kamins, Folkes & Fedorikhin 2009). A bonus earns its place only by answering the buyer's next objection ("and then?", "how do I practise?").
- **[M] Bundle buyers attribute the discount to the item they wanted least;** partitioned prices feel smaller. Show the total. (Morwitz, Greenleaf & Johnson 1998; Stremersch & Tellis 2002)
- **[F] Value-stack frameworks** from practitioner books (for expensive US services, no controlled tests) are useful as question lists: raise the dream outcome and perceived likelihood, cut time delay and effort. Keep: one clear promise, risk-transferring guarantee, a name that describes the mechanism. Drop: summed value without proof, decorative bonuses, manufactured scarcity. In low ticket, a big stack sounds implausible for the price. [F]

## Myths and numbers not to repeat
| Claim | Status | Say instead |
|---|---|---|
| "More bonuses, more perceived value" | [X] presenter's paradox | few strong items |
| "Order bumps convert 30-40%" | [F] vendor data | measure with a control |
| "Prices ending in 9 always sell more" | [M] small, unstable | fine-tuning after offer and price |
| "A high anchor doubles what they pay" | [M] weak replication | honest anchors help situate the price |
| "A decoy plan always pushes to the middle" | [M] fails outside the lab | test with real plans |
| "Long guarantees increase returns" | [X] they tend to reduce them | wide scope is what increases returns |
| "In the EU one checkout sentence removes the withdrawal right" | [X] needs the three steps, digital content only | follow the CRD steps |
| "The 30-day rule applies equally to everything in the EU" | [X] goods at EU level; services/digital per country | check national law |
| "Upsells must be 2-5x the front" | [F] one practitioner source | measure contribution per buyer |
| "Ask customers what they would pay" | [M] overestimates ~21% | test with real purchases |

## Book-derived rules (principles in my own words; full citations in `BIBLIOGRAPHY.md`)
1. **Value equation (Hormozi, *$100M Offers*, 2021) [F practitioner]:** perceived value rises with the size and believability of the outcome and falls with the delay and the effort the buyer must spend. Lever order for a weak offer: shorten time to first result, cut effort (done-for-you steps, templates), add proof of likelihood, only then add bonuses. Bonuses must be real and cost-aware; stacking junk lowers trust.
2. **Settle willingness to pay before building (Ramanujam and Tacke, *Monetizing Innovation*, 2016) [M]:** design the product around price research, not price around the finished product. Use a value metric the customer already understands (per seat, per project, per outcome), segment by willingness to pay, and offer 2-3 packages built from the features each segment values most. Stated-intent surveys overstate (see myths table): validate with real purchases.
3. **Value ladder (Brunson, *DotCom Secrets*, 2015) [F practitioner]:** a sequence from a low-risk entry offer to higher-ticket products, each step solving the next problem the buyer has. Rule: every step has its own honest promise; the entry offer earns trust, it is not a trap (EU limits in `eu-consumer-law.md`).
4. **Value proposition as a checklist (Osterwalder and Pigneur, *Business Model Generation*, 2010, building block 2) [F framework]:** the book lists the ways an offer can create value for one segment: newness, performance, customisation, getting a job done for the customer, design, brand or status, price, cost reduction, risk reduction, accessibility, convenience. Use it as a question list in the offer stack (`playbooks.md` 1): which two or three does this offer really carry, and does this segment pay for them? A group is a separate segment only if its needs justify a different offer, it is reached or served differently, it is clearly more or less profitable, or it pays for different things; then write one proposition per segment. No controlled evidence shows the canvas improves results; the book is from 2010 and does not contain the later value-proposition canvas.
5. **Revenue streams and pricing mechanisms (same book, building block 5 and the pricing table) [F framework]:** seven stream types: asset sale, usage fee, subscription fee, lending/renting/leasing, licensing, brokerage fee, advertising. Ask for each stream what the customer is really paying for and whether they would pay for it again. Fixed mechanisms are list price, feature-dependent, segment-dependent and volume-dependent; dynamic ones are negotiation, yield management, real-time market and auction. Per-person automatic prices carry EU disclosure duties (`eu-consumer-law.md` 3), and auctions with a countdown mix scarcity with rivalry (see rule 7).
6. **Freemium (same book, the "Free" patterns) [F]:** the book says typically fewer than 10% of free users ever pay, so the paying few subsidise the free base, and two numbers decide viability: cost to serve one free user and the free-to-paid conversion rate. The figure has no cited source here; measure your own and apply `metrics-and-testing.md`. "Free" must remain free to the user (`eu-consumer-law.md` 2).
7. **Reciprocity and scarcity in offers (Cialdini, *Influence*, ch. 2 and 7) [M]:** give a real first deliverable (sample lesson, diagnosis) before the paid ask; an unrequested gift moves people more than a conditional one, but a "free bonus" attached to a purchase is not a gift and can lower perceived value (presenter's paradox, above). Use one real smaller alternative, never a ladder of insistence (`bump-upsell-downsell.md`). Scarcity raises desire, not judged quality, so it never replaces proof; allow only limits that exist and say why (cohort capacity, a dated event, live support hours). Digital items have no stock-based scarcity, and invented deadlines are banned practices: legal limits in `eu-consumer-law.md` 6 stay in force. Rivalry plus a ticking clock makes buyers overpay and later regret it, which shows up as refunds (`diagnosis-playbook.md` Tree F).

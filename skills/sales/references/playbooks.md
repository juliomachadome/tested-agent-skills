# Playbooks: discovery, qualification, objections, follow-up, proposal, negotiation

Method evidence: **[C]** consensus, **[M]** single or old study, **[F]** practitioner. Scripts are patterns in plain words; adapt, do not recite. Never claim a method "proves" a close rate.

## 1. Discovery call script (SPIN + Gap) [M]
Time box 30-45 min. The buyer talks about 60% of it. Prepare: company, role, trigger event, three hypotheses, the one number you want out of the call.

| Phase | Minutes | Purpose | Example questions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Open | 3 | agenda, time, permission, outcome | "I'd like to understand how you handle X today, see whether a fit exists, and agree what to do next either way. Okay? Anything to add to the agenda?" |
| Situation (few; researched ones skipped) | 5 | facts you cannot find elsewhere | "Walk me through how X works now: who does what, with which tools, how often?" |
| Problem | 8 | difficulty, dissatisfaction, trigger | "What is hardest about it? What made you look at this now? What have you tried?" |
| Implication (the work) | 10 | size of the problem in time, money, risk, people | "When it fails, what happens next? How often? Who gets involved? What did the last incident cost in hours or money? What does it do to [their goal]?" |
| Need-payoff / future state | 5 | the buyer says the value aloud | "If this were solved, what would be different? How would you measure it? What would that be worth?" |
| Decision process | 5 | EB, criteria, steps, budget path, competitors | "Who else will have a view? How were similar purchases approved? Which steps come after the business case (security, legal, vendor onboarding)? What is the budget line? What else are you considering, including doing nothing?" |
| Next step | 3 | dated, mutual | "Based on this, a fit looks [likely/unclear]. The sensible next step is X with Y by [date]. Does that work for you?" |

Gap record (fill it live, send back for confirmation): current state | future state | gap | cost of the gap per year (buyer's numbers) | cost of the fix | who confirmed. Recap email within 24 h: what we heard (their words), the number, next step with date, and an invitation to correct anything.
Rules: no pitch before minute 20 unless asked; one question at a time; silence after the number; if the pain is small, say so and stop.
Founder / non-technical buyer variant (**The Mom Test** idea [F]): ask about past behaviour and real spend, not opinions about your idea; "when did this last happen and what did you do?" beats "would you use this?".
Question types, implicit vs explicit needs, small vs large sale, advance vs continuation, objection prevention and the limits of the SPIN evidence: section 7.

## 2. Qualification scorecard (MEDDICC) [F]
Score each letter 0 (unknown), 1 (hypothesis from the seller), 2 (buyer-stated, not written), 3 (buyer-confirmed in writing or by a second person). Evidence is what a stranger could audit in the CRM.

| Letter | Question | Good evidence (3) | Weak (0-1) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Metrics | What number moves, by how much, by when? | buyer's own baseline and target in an email or the business case | seller-estimated ROI the buyer never saw |
| Economic buyer | Who can sign and release money, and have we spoken? | met or on a call; they stated priority and budget source | "my boss decides" with no access |
| Decision criteria | What must be true to choose a vendor (technical, commercial, risk)? | written list, ranked, seen in an RFP or scorecard | "price and quality" |
| Decision process | Steps, owners, dates from now to signature? | mutual plan with dates; legal and security steps named | "after the summer" |
| Identify pain | Is the problem urgent and owned? | quantified gap, a consequence and a deadline, in their words | "nice to have", no trigger |
| Champion | Who sells for us when we are not in the room, with influence and a stake? | has given non-public information, arranged access, tested by a request they fulfilled | friendly contact with no power or no follow-through |
| Competition | Alternatives, including status quo? | named, with the buyer's view of strengths and gaps | "no competition" (it is almost always the status quo) |

Reading it: total below 10 of 21 at proposal stage = not forecastable; any 0 on Economic buyer, Pain or Decision process at proposal stage = go back one stage. Use it as a question list, not as a predictor; no study validates the score itself **[F]**. Origin note: the framework is attributed to an enterprise software sales team in the 1990s and extended later; see bibliography for what could and could not be verified.

## 3. Objection map (price, timing, trust, status quo)
Prevent first: objections raised early or about value usually mean a solution was offered before the need was built (section 7.5). Pattern **[F]**: acknowledge without agreeing, ask one question to find the real concern, answer with a fact, comparison or small test, check ("does that cover it?"), agree the next step. Never invent urgency or a rival offer.

| Objection | Real concern (guess, then ask) | Ethical response pattern | Do not |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Too expensive" | no proof of value; budget ceiling; compared to a different scope | "Compared with what? If price were not an issue, would this be the right fit?" then gap-vs-price arithmetic (`deal-diagnosis.md`), then options: smaller scope, phased start, longer term for lower rate | cut price first; claim a "special price today" that is not real |
| "We have no budget" | no budget line; priority low | "How do similar things get funded? What would need to be true to fund this in the next cycle?" | pressure a person with no authority |
| "Not now" / timing | no trigger; fear of change | "What would make it a priority? What happens if you wait six months?" and set a dated check-in or a trigger | invent a deadline |
| "Send me information" | soft no or no champion | "Happy to; what would you look for so I send the right page? Could we take 15 minutes with [decision maker] to walk it?" | send a brochure and wait |
| "Need to talk to my boss / committee" | champion unequipped | "What will they ask? Let me give you a one-page summary and join the call if useful." | go around the champion without telling them |
| Trust: "Who else uses it? You are small/new" | risk of choosing wrong | give two verifiable references, a pilot with exit criteria, escrow or uptime commitment, security documents; accept the risk is real | invent logos or testimonials |
| Trust: security or legal | procurement gate | send documents early; name the owner on your side; dated commitment | promise features that do not exist |
| Status quo: "we use X" / "we do it in spreadsheets" | switching cost, habit | "What does it do well? What does it not? What would a good switch look like?" side-by-side pilot, migration plan | attack the incumbent |
| Competitor is cheaper | scope or risk difference | ask for the scope list; compare line by line; trade scope for price | match a number you have not seen |

## 4. Follow-up cadence (after a proposal or silence) [F]
5-8 touches, 3-4 weeks, two channels, each touch adds something. Stop at an explicit no or unsubscribe. Respect outreach law (consent, opt-out) and the buyer's time zone.

| Day | Channel | Value in the touch |
|---|---|---|
| 0 (same day as meeting) | email | recap in their words, the number, next step with date |
| 2 | email | one useful asset tied to their stated problem (checklist, short case, benchmark with source) |
| 5 | phone or LinkedIn message | answer the question they are likely to have (security, rollout, price drivers) |
| 9 | email | short question: "Is it clearer to do A or B first?" with a calendar link |
| 14 | email to champion | a one-page internal summary they can forward, plus an offer to join the committee call |
| 21 | email (other contact, copied openly) | new angle: a risk of waiting, a relevant change (regulation, price, season), only if true |
| 28 | email | break-up note with a date; offer to reopen later |
Never "just checking in". Subject lines state the content. Log every touch and the reply.

## 5. Proposal template (one decision page, then detail) [F]
Walk the buyer through it live before sending; a proposal that surprises is a failed discovery.
1. **Problem** in their words, with their numbers (from the gap record).
2. **Outcome** and how it is measured, date of first result.
3. **Solution** in plain language; what you will do and what the buyer must do.
4. **Scope**: in, out, assumptions, dependencies, acceptance criteria.
5. **Plan**: phases and dates; mutual plan with owners.
6. **Price options**: three packages (minimal, recommended, extended) with what drives each; payment terms; term; what a change costs. Show the cost of the problem next to the price.
7. **Risks and mitigation**: top 3 (data, adoption, timeline), what you do about each, what you ask of the buyer.
8. **Terms**: validity date (a real one), SLAs, data protection, exit.
9. **Next step**: signature or decision meeting, date, who. 
Appendix: references that can be called, security pack, team CVs.

## 6. Negotiation (Fisher and Ury; Voss) [C] / [M]
Prepare before the call: your BATNA (best alternative if no deal), the buyer's likely BATNA, your walk-away, the variables (price, term, volume, scope, payment date, references, exclusivity, onboarding effort), and a ranked list of what each variable costs you and is worth to them.
- **Interests over positions** (Fisher and Ury): ask why the number matters; several solutions often satisfy both sides. Use objective criteria (market rates, standards, the buyer's own policy) to settle disputes [C].
- **Anchors**: the first number frames the range; anchor from your documented value and your list, with a reason, not from the lowest you would accept. If they anchor low, do not counter with a number; ask "how did you arrive at that?" and return to scope **[M]**.
- **Concessions**: trade, never give. Pattern "If you can do X (term, volume, date), I can do Y." Concede in decreasing size so the buyer reads you are near your limit; never concede without something back.
- **Voss tools** [M]: label the emotion ("it sounds like the budget is under pressure"), mirror the last words to draw detail, calibrated "how/what" questions ("How would we make this work for your budget?"), summarise until you hear "that's right". Treat them as listening discipline, not manipulation; drop any tactic the buyer would find deceptive.
- **Do not**: invent a competing offer, a deadline, an internal "approval limit", or a "last price"; disguise a deadline as a favour. A bluff caught ends the relationship and in B2G may break law (`b2g-procurement.md`).
- Close: write the agreed terms the same day, ask for confirmation, and set the start date.

## 7. SPIN in depth (Rackham) [M]
Evidence base: field observation of tens of thousands of calls by Rackham's research firm, correlational, mostly B2B and capital-goods type sales, 1980s; the author's company sells SPIN training, so effect sizes are indicative. He himself calls SPIN a description of how good sellers sell on a good day, not a script (Rackham, SPIN Selling, ch. 4).

**7.1 Is it a large sale? Decide before choosing the style.** Signs of a large sale (ch. 1 and 3): money and risk are high for the buyer personally (a bad choice is visible and can cost a job), several people influence it, the need grows slowly, and the relationship continues after the signature, so buyer and seller become hard to separate. In a small, one-visit sale, pressure and quick closes can work; the same moves lose ground as the decision grows (7.4). Calibrate by the buyer's perceived risk, not the list price (Rackham says perceived size is subjective, ch. 2); my example: a EUR 200 monthly tool bought by one user is small even in B2B.

**7.2 Four question types and what the data said (ch. 4).**
| Type | Asks about | Pattern in the research | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Situation | facts, background | more of them in failed calls; novices overuse them because they feel safe; buyers tire fast | few; do the desk research first so none is asked that a website answers |
| Problem | difficulties, dissatisfaction | strongly tied to success in small sales, weakly in large ones; supplies the raw material | find 3 plausible problems before the call and write one real question for each |
| Implication | consequences of the problem on cost, people, other areas, customers | tied to success in large sales and with decision-makers; hardest to ask (about 1 in 20 questions in the average call) | plan them: write a problem, list what it causes, turn each cause into a question |
| Need-payoff | value or usefulness of solving it | tied to success in large sales; the buyer says the benefit aloud | after the problem feels big, not before |
Sad/happy test (a remark from a child that Rackham's team adopted, ch. 4): implication questions dwell on the problem, need-payoff questions on the solution. Sequence S, P, I, N, but jump when the buyer volunteers a want, and go back to S when you need context. Open vs closed questions did not discriminate winners (ch. 4): what matters is whether the question touches something the buyer cares about. One cultural exception: in Japan problem questions were harder to ask yet still tied to better results (ch. 4).
Example of an implication chain (adapt the numbers): "Hard to use, you said. Which effect does that have on output? ... on training cost? ... on overtime and outsourcing? ... on quality and delivery dates?" Close by summarising the buyer's own words, then ask the need-payoff: "If this were fixed, what would change for you?".

**7.3 Implicit vs explicit needs (ch. 3).** Implicit need = a stated problem, difficulty or dissatisfaction ("the current process is slow"). Explicit need = a stated want or intention to act ("we need a faster process"; "we are replacing it next year"). In the study of small sales, successful calls had about twice the implicit needs of failed ones; in large sales the implicit count did not separate them (about 4.2 vs 3.9), while explicit needs did (about 2.7 vs 1.3). So: count explicit statements as buying signals; treat "they agree they have a problem" as only the start; the job of the questions is to turn implicit into explicit. Value equation (ch. 3): the buyer buys when perceived problem size exceeds perceived cost, and cost includes risk, effort and personal exposure, not only price. If you offer a solution while the problem is small, the buyer hears a price that does not fit and objects.

**7.4 Advance vs continuation (ch. 2).** Four outcomes of a call: order, advance (the customer does something that moves the decision: a trial, a demo with technical staff, an introduction to a higher decision-maker, access to another part of the account), continuation (talk continues, nothing agreed, e.g. "great presentation, keep in touch") and refusal. Rackham counts continuations as failures because compliments are polite exits; judge a call by the buyer's action, not their words. Set the call objective as a customer action before you go ("the engineering lead attends a trial", not "build rapport", "collect information"). A seller who cannot name a realistic advance has nothing to close; the problem is the objective, not the closing technique. Closing study (ch. 2): after closing training in a photo shop, low-value sales closed slightly faster and slightly more often (72% to 76%, not statistically significant), but high-value sales fell from 42% to 33%; in a small survey, 34 of 54 professional buyers said recognising closing techniques made them less likely to buy. Four moves that did work in large sales: invest most time in the investigation stage; before proposing a step, ask whether any concern is still open (so a concern arrives as a request, not a fight); summarise the benefits in the buyer's words; then propose the most realistic next step that still moves the deal, stated rather than begged for ("The logical next step would be that you and your finance lead see it running; shall I arrange it for the 12th?"). Add the date and the owner (next-step language in `deal-diagnosis.md`).

**7.5 Features, advantages, benefits, and objection prevention (ch. 5-6).** Rackham's labels (they differ from most sales courses): a feature is a fact about the offer; an advantage shows how the offer can help in general; a benefit shows how it meets an explicit need the buyer already stated. In large sales, features tended to raise price concern (and in a premium offer a feature list can make the buyer ask whether it is worth it), advantages tended to draw objections, and benefits drew support (ch. 6, fig. 6.2). Calls with more objections were less successful: about 6% of buyer behaviour in calls ending in an order or advance vs about 13% in refusals (694 calls, ch. 6). In one company, eight sellers with high objection counts were trained only in SPIN and benefit statements, with no objection-handling content, and their objections per selling hour fell 55% (ch. 6, a small case, no control group stated). Practical reading: (a) objections in the first minutes mean you pitched before asking; (b) "too expensive", "not worth the change", "we are happy as we are" are value objections, so go back to implication and need-payoff questions instead of defending the price; (c) a real mismatch (the offer cannot meet the need) will still produce objections; prevention is not a promise they vanish. Objection scripts remain in section 3.

**7.6 Coach the buyer to sell inside (ch. 4).** In large sales much of the selling happens when you are absent. Need-payoff questions make the buyer explain the benefit in their own words and in the needs they know best (their business), which is better rehearsal than hearing your feature list; ask who they will need to convince and what that person cares about, then help with a one-page summary of the buyer's own reasons.

**7.7 Opening the call (ch. 7).** Personal small talk and rapport helped in small rural sales but was not related to success in larger urban ones; professional buyers complain about sellers who waste their time and some distrust personal hooks. An opening benefit statement showed no relationship to success in 300+ larger calls; sellers who opened every call the same way did worse than those who varied, and a benefit opening invites product and price questions too early. Job of the opening: say who you are, why you are here (without product detail) and get permission to ask questions; keep it under about a fifth of the call. Spend preparation time on questions, not on the first sentence.

## 8. Rapport and listening signals: the ethical subset (Schafer and Karlins) [F]
Source: a former FBI behavioural analyst's book (The Like Switch), built on recruiting and interviewing suspects and foreign officials; evidence is the author's experience plus borrowed psychology (similarity attraction, reciprocity, mirroring), not controlled sales studies. Some specifics are unverified (for example the explanations for head tilts or lip signs). Rackham found personal rapport matters less as the sale grows (7.7) and buyers often say they like a seller but buy the cheaper offer, so rapport lowers friction but does not create a need.
- **Friendship formula (ch. 1):** liking grows with proximity, frequency, duration and intensity of contact in a non-threatening setting. Frequency and duration trade off (people who meet rarely meet for long). Sales translation: several short, useful, low-pressure touches over weeks (a useful asset, a short call after an event) are more natural than one long cold pitch. Applies to outreach cadence (section 4).
- **Friendly signals (ch. 2):** brief eye contact with a real smile, a quick eyebrow lift on greeting, an open and slightly tilted head posture, leaning in when interested. Use as normal courtesy, not as a technique; do not stare (more than a second or two reads as a threat) and do not fake the smile. Treat the buyer's signals (leaning back, turning away, lips pressed, hesitation before speaking) as a prompt to ask, never as proof: "You paused at the timeline; what worries you there?". Reliable reading of a single gesture is not established, and the book gives no tests.
- **Empathic statements (ch. 3):** a short statement of what you heard about their situation or feeling, phrased "so you ..." and tentative, keeps the focus on them and invites correction ("So the audit date is what is driving the timing?"). Do not repeat their exact words (it reads as mockery) and do not say "I understand how you feel".
- **Do not make the buyer wrong (ch. 5):** a correct but blunt correction ("you have been doing this wrong") triggers defence; ask for their view or advice ("I would value your view on a point about the process") and leave them a graceful way to change their mind. Prefer "we" about shared goals; credit them for ideas you built on.
- **Listening (ch. 5):** pause a second after they finish (some people think before speaking); do not interrupt; note what they emphasise.
- **Similarity and reciprocity (ch. 4):** real common ground (sector, city, a shared problem) speeds trust; do not invent a shared background (the book itself warns the lie backfires). A small useful favour before a request is normal reciprocity; if it feels like leverage, it is manipulation, and the buyer's compliance rules may ban gifts.
Excluded on purpose: arousal-misattribution dates, bait to provoke curiosity, flattery, touch, covert elicitation of confessions, and using signals to hide your own aim. The buyer is a counterpart who should know who you are and what you want.

## 9. Competitive intelligence within ethical limits (Nolan) [F]
Source: a 1999 practitioner book by a business-intelligence consultant (Confidential); its recommended method is elicitation, often with the source unaware; part of it is gray or worse. Use the intelligence cycle and the protection ideas, not the covert tactics.
- **Cycle (ch. 9):** start from the decision that needs information (tasking), break it into specific questions, collect from open sources first and then people, process and rate the reliability of each source, analyse against the question (and never bend the analysis to confirm what leadership already believes), report. For a sales team: the decision is "how do we position and price against X in segment Y", not "tell me everything about X".
- **What is clearly legitimate:** public sources (their site, price pages, job ads, filings, release notes, review sites, conference talks and trade-show booths), customers and partners who volunteer information about what they saw, your own staff's debriefs from open conversations, and win/loss interviews with the buyer's consent. The industry code the book quotes (SCIP, ch. 8) says: comply with the law, state your name and organisation before an interview, respect confidentiality requests, follow company policy.
- **Do not:** use a false identity or invented research project (pretexting), pose as a buyer to extract a rival's terms, eavesdrop, bribe, ask a buyer or ex-employee to hand over a rival's proposal, NDA-covered material or a trade secret, or reuse information you know was leaked. Some of the book's own examples (ruse interviews, a listener posted in hotel washrooms) go beyond the ethics code it quotes and sit close to what its legal appendix warns about. In the US, the Economic Espionage Act of 1996 (App. B) covers taking or obtaining trade secrets by theft, fraud, artifice or deception, with criminal penalties for individuals and officers; EU, UK and Brazilian trade-secret rules were not read for this skill (ask counsel). In a public tender never use another bidder's information (`b2g-procurement.md`).
- **What a seller can ask a buyer:** "Which criteria did you use to compare? What did the other offer do better? What would have to change for us to win?" Do not ask for their documents or prices.
- **Protect your own side (Part III, ch. 14-17):** list the few topics that hurt you if leaked (price floors, roadmap dates, named customers, churn data); train reps that an unusual stream of friendly questions about those topics may be elicitation, that giving a little to get a little ("quid pro quo") is the standard way to extract more, and that a reply like "I can't share that, but here is what is public" is professional. Check what your site, proposals and event staff already reveal.
- **Output for a deal:** a one-page note: decision, 5-8 claims with source type and reliability (public, buyer-reported, inferred), what is unknown, what we will say in the call, what we will not say. Mark inferences as inferences.

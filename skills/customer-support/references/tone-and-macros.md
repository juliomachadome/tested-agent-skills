# Tone and macros

Evidence tags as in SKILL.md. Principles come from effort research (Dixon, Toman and DeLisi, vendor-led, indicative **[M]**) and practitioner experience **[F]**. For full reply recipes see `playbooks.md`.

## Tone by situation
| Situation | Do | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Angry | shortest path to action first; one-line acknowledgement tied to a fact; one concrete step and a time | "calm down", defensiveness, three apologies |
| Confused | plain steps, one at a time, the expected result after each step; offer screenshot or call if the second reply fails | jargon, long lists |
| Our fault | say it plainly, what we are doing, what the customer gets (fix, credit if policy allows) | passive voice ("mistakes were made") |
| Not our fault or not possible | say no in the first line, one reason, nearest alternative | long justification, blaming the customer |
| Grief, health or safety mention | human tone, escalate to a lead, skip the macro | upbeat templates |
| VIP or high value | same honesty, faster routing, named owner | different truth for different customers |
| Non-native or very formal customer | match register, shorter sentences, no idioms | slang, sarcasm |
Positive wording helps ("here is what I can do") **[M]**; do not use it to hide a no.

## Macro design
Every macro has: name, owner, trigger (when to use), "do not use when", placeholders (name, order id, date, amount), body under about 120 words, tags to apply, last review date. Draft rules: first line answers or acts; one ask per reply; numbered steps if more than two; date and timezone for any promise; no filler ("I hope this finds you well", "we value your feedback"); read aloud before publishing; test with five real tickets and check that the placeholders fill.
- Never auto-send macros for emotional, money, privacy, safety or cancellation topics; an agent edits them first.
- Personalise one sentence at least: the specific fact from the ticket.
- A real person's name only if the agent is that person.
- Mix macro and article: for a how-to, link the article rather than pasting 10 steps; keep the text in one place.

## Maintenance
Monthly: top 10 macros by use and by CSAT; delete or rewrite those with low CSAT or high reopen rate; merge duplicates; retire those an article now answers; check for outdated prices, policy numbers, URLs. Quarterly: read all macros aloud, check consistency with current policy and law. Keep a change log (what, why, who). Track "macro used and reopened within 7 days" as a quality signal.

## Skeleton (generic)
1. One-line answer or action. 2. What happened or what we did. 3. What happens next and when. 4. What we need from you, if anything (numbered, all at once). 5. Close with how to reach us on this same thread.

## Examples of macro entries (generic)
- Name: double charge. Trigger: customer reports two charges for one order. Do not use when: charges are two different orders, or the customer disputes it with the bank already (go to a lead). Body: "I checked: you were charged [amount] twice on [date]. I have refunded the second charge; your bank usually shows it within [N] business days. Nothing else is needed from you. If it is not there by [date], reply to this message and I will trace it." Tags: billing, duplicate.
- Name: known issue. Trigger: ticket matches an open incident. Body: facts, workaround, next update time, status link.

## Nonviolent Communication lens (Rosenberg) [F]
Source: Rosenberg, Nonviolent Communication (ch. 1, 3-8 and 10) and Living Nonviolent Communication (ch. 2-3). A practitioner model from workshops and mediations; evidence is stories, not trials, and its spiritual framing does not transfer to a ticket queue. Take the thinking (separate facts from judgments, find the need, make concrete requests, listen before fixing); do not paste its vocabulary into replies: the author says the essence is awareness of four things, not particular words (ch. 1), and "I sense you feel..." formulas read as therapy in a billing ticket.
**The four parts (ch. 1):** what we observe (without evaluation), what we feel, which need is behind the feeling, what we request. Use them to read a message and to write a reply.

**1. Read the message in four parts (ch. 3-6).** Split an angry ticket: *observation* (two charges of 29 on 3 March; no reply in 3 days), *evaluation* ("you are thieves"), *feeling* (angry, worried), *need* (money back, to be able to rely on the company, to be heard), *request* (refund, an answer today). Answer the facts, the need and the request; do not reply to the label. Feelings written as "I feel ignored/cheated/manipulated" are interpretations of our behaviour (ch. 4): translate to a feeling (anxious, distrustful) and a need (reliability, clarity, respect) for your own notes. In internal notes record what the customer wrote or did, not an evaluation ("rude", "difficult", "abusive"): quote the words, date and channel (ch. 3: mixing observation and evaluation makes the other hear criticism).

**2. Four options when a message hurts (ch. 5).** Blame yourself (over-apologise, give in), blame the customer (defend, correct their tone), notice your own feeling and need (a pause, a colleague, a break), or tune in to theirs. Take the third to settle and the fourth to reply. The customer's behaviour is the stimulus of your reaction, not its cause; it keeps agents from taking insults personally (a burnout lever, `diagnosis-playbook.md` Tree J).

**3. Empathy before advice, but do not skip the action (ch. 7).** The book lists (crediting a colleague) habits that block listening: advising, one-upping, educating, consoling, telling a similar story, shutting down ("cheer up"), sympathising, interrogating, explaining and correcting. In support these look like: "As per policy...", "You should have...", "Don't worry", five questions in a row, "That is not what the page says". Reconcile with the first-line rule (`SKILL.md` 2): open with the fact and the action, then one tentative line about what the customer is up against and wants, only when the first message carried real emotion. Example: "You were charged twice on 3 March and had no answer for three days, and you need this money back this week; I have refunded the second charge today and will confirm by Friday 17:00." Check understanding by reflecting the point back when the case is complicated ("So the invoice is right but the date is wrong; is that it?"), and appreciate the correction if they say no (ch. 6). Ask before reassuring or fixing when the customer may only want to be heard (ch. 7); with grief or illness, listen first and hand to a lead.

**4. Requests, not demands (ch. 6; Living NVC ch. 2).** When you need something from the customer, ask for a concrete, doable action in positive words, with the reason: "Could you send a screenshot of the error screen? It shows me which server answered." not "Provide more details" or "Do not use the old link". A request becomes a demand if you criticise or press when they decline; accept "no" and offer another way. Vague requests ("be more careful", "cooperate") do not tell anyone what to do. In a group or a B2B thread, ask what response they want from you ("What would a good result look like by Friday?").

**5. Saying no (ch. 8 on hearing a no; Living NVC ch. 2 on needs versus strategies).** A refund or a feature is a strategy; the need behind it may be met another way (money back, credit, a fix, time). Say no in the first line, name the need or value behind your limit instead of "that's the policy" (fairness for all customers, account security), say what you can do, and ask what they need it for if the alternative does not fit. Example: "I cannot refund the annual plan after 60 days (we treat every customer the same way). I can move you to the monthly plan from today and credit the unused 4 months to it. Would that cover what you need?" Do not use "need" language to talk a customer out of a legal right (`refunds-consumer-law.md`).

**6. Apology (Living NVC ch. 3).** Rosenberg opposes apology as a moral admission that someone must suffer, and prefers naming what happened, the need it failed to meet and regret without shame. The skill keeps the one specific apology (`SKILL.md` 2), because customers and consumer-law practice expect it; take the idea of the best form: name the event, the effect on the customer's need, and the repair; no self-punishing wording ("we are terrible"), no excuses, no guilt for the customer. Do not state liability beyond facts in sensitive incidents without legal review. Example: "We charged you twice on 3 March, and that left you short for the week. I am sorry; I have refunded the second charge and added a check for duplicate payments."

**7. Anger and boundaries (ch. 10).** Anger usually sits on top of a judgment ("they are cheats") and an unmet need (to be treated reliably). Treat it as information: ask what happened and what they need. For your own anger, name your need (respect, safety) and pause or hand off. A boundary is a need plus a request: "I want to keep working on this and I need the messages to be free of insults; can we continue that way?" (the abusive-customer ladder stays in `playbooks.md` 1.7).

**8. Voice of customer.** Next to topic tags, record the need behind the complaint (predictability, clarity, control over money, respect, time). It points the product team to the cause, not only to the category.
